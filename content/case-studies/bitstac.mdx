## Context and problem

Importers and SMEs trading between China and Africa face slow, expensive and unpredictable cross-border payments. Traditional rails add days, intermediaries and FX friction, and small businesses have the least leverage against them. Any alternative also has to satisfy compliance requirements, or it cannot be offered at all.

The business problem was twofold: make settlement fast and low-cost without weakening trust, and turn it into a platform that businesses would put real trade volume through.

## Who it's for

SMEs and importers paying suppliers across the China–Africa corridor, and the early enterprise clients who needed to integrate payments into their own systems. Their job to be done is to pay a supplier quickly, at a predictable cost, with confidence the money arrives and the counterparty is legitimate.

## Discovery and key insight

I built partnerships with African SME communities and trade networks early, so adoption and market-fit assumptions were tested with real traders before they became roadmap commitments.

**The insight that shaped the product:** speed and cost get a business to try the platform, but trust and compliance clarity decide whether it stays. Onboarding therefore could not be treated as a hurdle to minimise. It had to be designed as part of the product's credibility.

## Options considered and decisions

| Decision | The alternative | What I chose | Trade-off accepted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Settlement rail | Traditional correspondent rails vs stablecoin settlement | USDT and USDC | Friction moves from FX and intermediaries to wallet, custody and on-chain design, which I shaped directly with engineering |
| Compliance model | One heavy gate for everyone vs tiered checks | Tiered KYB with fraud, AML and cybersecurity checkpoints | Proportionate risk assessment without forcing the heaviest checks on every business on day one |
| Launch sequence | Broad SME release vs enterprise-first | Early enterprise clients first | A small set of demanding clients exposed reliability and API gaps before they could reach the wider SME base |
| Distribution | Paid acquisition vs community-led | Partnerships with African SME communities and trade networks | Adoption came with built-in trust and market validation from the communities themselves |

## AI approach

Bitstac was not an AI-led product, and I did not force AI into it. The technical judgment here sat in blockchain and risk architecture.

I applied working knowledge of crypto and blockchain fundamentals, including wallet architecture, on-chain transaction flows and stablecoin settlement mechanics, to guide engineering trade-offs. Automated risk signals entered the product through the fraud detection and AML screening checkpoints in the KYB flow. I worked closely with engineering on API design and the technical decisions that kept the platform reliable as volume grew.

## What I'd do differently

I would instrument the onboarding funnel by KYB tier from the first release, so the cost of every compliance checkpoint in drop-off was visible next to the risk it removed. I would also bring compliance and engineering into discovery sooner. On a regulated product, the fastest roadmap is the one where the constraint is understood before the design, not after it.
